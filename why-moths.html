Why Moths, Not Butterflies

People ask this as though it were a strange preference, like liking the rain. It is not. Here is the case, in four parts.

1. There are vastly more of them

Butterflies are a small branch of the Lepidoptera that happened to take up the day shift. Depending on how you count, moths outnumber them roughly nine to one. Every field guide that puts butterflies on the cover and squeezes the moths into an appendix at the back has the proportions exactly backwards.

2. The colours are better, and harder won

A butterfly is coloured to be seen. A moth is coloured to be missed, and that is a far more interesting design problem. Bark, lichen, dead leaf, bird droppings, a snapped twig — the good ones are not merely brown, they are a specific brown, matched to a specific surface, and they know how to sit on it. When a moth does go bright, it is usually hiding the bright part under something dull and saving it for the instant a beak arrives.

“Anyone can be beautiful in daylight with an audience. Try being beautiful in a way that has to work at two in the morning on a fence post.”

3. They do the night shift of pollination

Whole categories of plants are moth-pollinated — the pale ones, the strongly scented ones, the ones that open at dusk. They are doing this while nobody is watching and getting no credit whatsoever, and recent work suggests they may move pollen further and between more plants in a night than bees do in a day.

4. You can actually meet them

This is the real reason. A butterfly is a thing you chase across a paddock and lose. A moth comes to you. Put a light against a sheet and the local Lepidoptera will present themselves in order, all night, and sit still while you look. It is the only branch of natural history where the wildlife makes the appointment.

If that has done its work, the species list is where to start, and the field notes show what a real season looks like — which is to say, mostly nothing, punctuated.

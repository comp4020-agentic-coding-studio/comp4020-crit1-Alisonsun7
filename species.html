The Species

These are the ones I keep coming back to. Not the rarest — I am not a serious person about rarity — but the ones that reward looking. The table is the short version; the notes underneath are why each one is here.

Six moths worth staying up for
Moth Wingspan When Look for
Bogong 40–50 mm Spring, moving Dark arrowhead at rest; arrives in numbers or not at all
Privet hawk-moth 90–120 mm Warm nights, Dec–Feb Pink and black barred body under grey forewings
Emperor gum moth 80–150 mm Late spring Four eyespots; enormous and completely unbothered
Magpie moth 30–40 mm Day-flying, spring Black on white, flies badly on purpose
Wattle goat moth up to 160 mm Summer, rarely Bark-grey, heavy, the size of a small bird
Plume moth 15–25 mm All season Holds as a capital T; looks like a dried grass stem

The notes

Bogong

The famous one, and the one that has gone quiet. They used to arrive in the millions on the way to the high country, and there were years recently when they simply did not. Seeing one now is a different feeling than it was twenty years ago: less nuisance, more relief.

Privet hawk-moth

A jet fighter. Holds its wings swept back and can hover at a flower like it has read about hummingbirds and decided to have a go. If one lands on the sheet the whole night is justified.

Emperor gum moth

Enormous, furred, and entirely harmless — the adults have no working mouthparts and live about a fortnight on what the caterpillar stored. The eyespots are not for you; they are for something with a beak that was about to commit.

Plume moth

My favourite, and the one nobody notices. At rest it rolls its wings into two narrow spars held out at right angles to the body, so it reads as a bit of dead grass that happens to be on your wall. Then it unrolls, and there are feathers.

More as the season turns. See the field notes for what has actually shown up, which is a shorter list than this one.
